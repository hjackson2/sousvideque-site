Doneness chart (preference guide)

This is a doneness preference chart from Anova and Serious Eats, not a USDA or FDA consumer minimum. Every row is below the agencies’ 145°F fin-fish minimum. Like eggs, salmon’s softly setting proteins change a lot with small temperature shifts: Anova and Serious Eats both say the difference between salmon cooked to 120°F / 49°C and 130°F / 54°C is vast. Their personal favorite is 115°F / 46°C — firm enough that it tastes “cooked,” but still extremely moist and buttery-smooth, with just a hint of flakiness. Anova’s printed traditional table lists 30 minutes to 1 hour at each of these bath temperatures. In the same guides, both also give a thickness rule: 30 to 45 minutes for thinner fillets (Serious Eats and the Anova recipe: about 1 inch thick or less), and 45 minutes to 1 hour for extra-thick fillets (up to about 2 inches). They warn that fish can turn watery and mushy if left in the bath too long at low temperatures, and dry and chalky when left too long above 125°F / 52°C.

That is a different Anova chart, not a substitute for the five-row table above. The Anova Precision Oven “Sous Vide Express” salmon chart is oven-only and is not used here.

Thickness and pasteurization (Baldwin)

Douglas Baldwin separates heat-through time from pasteurization time. Example from his Table 2.2 (thawed, slab-like, to 1°F / 0.5°C less than the bath): a 25 mm (~1 in) piece needs about 1¼ hours to heat through; a 50 mm (~2 in) piece about 3½ hours. He warns: do not use heat-through tables alone to claim pasteurization. For fish he publishes Table 3.1, pasteurization times for a one-million-to-one reduction of Listeria in fin fish, starting from 41°F / 5°C in a 131–140°F / 55–60°C bath. He uses a longer D-value for fatty fish such as salmon than for lean fish such as cod. His separate “Poached Fish” recipe (cod, snapper, and similar) cooks fillets in a 131°F / 55°C to 141°F / 60.5°C bath for at least the Table 3.1 times. A few fatty-fish (salmon) rows from Table 3.1:

Baldwin’s own preference points for fish in Table 2.1 — rare 108°F / 42°C, medium-rare 122°F / 50°C, medium 140°F / 60°C — are a separate chart from Anova and Serious Eats. In the mi-cuit recipe he cooks rare and medium-rare salmon for only 15–20 minutes, and pasteurizes medium salmon using Table 3.1.

Unpasteurized-fish caution

Caution: The Anova/Serious Eats preference temps, and Baldwin’s rare and medium-rare mi-cuit temps, are not pasteurizing cooks. Baldwin writes that salmon mi-cuit should never be served to immune-compromised individuals, because those low temperatures are not sufficient to reduce foodborne pathogens or parasites. He notes that the parasite Anisakis simplex may exceed 75% prevalence in various types of fresh U.S. commercial wild salmon, and he recommends either freezing the fish (below −4°F / −20°C for at least 24 hours) to kill parasites, or pasteurizing it using Table 3.1. Elsewhere he writes that raw or unpasteurized food must never be served to highly susceptible or immune-compromised people, and that only immune-competent people should eat unpasteurized food, knowing the risks. FDA says people at greater risk — including pregnant women, children, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems — should avoid raw or undercooked fish or shellfish (and foods that contain them). If you are cooking for someone in those groups, or you are unsure your bath and thermometer are accurate, follow the USDA/FDA 145°F consumer minimum, or use a Baldwin Table 3.1 pasteurization hold.

Sources: Baldwin; FDA — Selecting and Serving Fresh and Frozen Seafood Safely; FDA — Cooking (Food Safety for Moms-to-Be). Serious Eats notes that salmon isn’t great cooked above the pasteurization point it cites (130°F / 54°C), and for safety strongly recommends against letting more than three hours pass between the time you start cooking and the time you serve, unless you chill immediately after cooking.

USDA / FDA consumer minima (traditional cook)

USDA FSIS consumer guidance for fish and shellfish is a minimum internal temperature of 145°F / 62.8°C, measured with a food thermometer. FDA’s Safe Food Handling chart says the same for fin fish: 145°F, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork. FoodSafety.gov (a federal food-safety site) lists fish such as salmon at 145°F / 63°C, or cook until the flesh is no longer translucent and separates easily with a fork. FDA also tells moms-to-be to cook finfish to an internal temperature of 145°F / 63°C. Leftovers: USDA FSIS and FDA both say reheat to 165°F (FSIS: 165°F / 73.9°C). That is traditional-cook consumer guidance—not a sous vide time-at-temp schedule. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours (1 hour if the air is above 90°F); keep the fridge at 40°F or below; leftovers keep 3 to 4 days in the fridge or 3 to 4 months in the freezer (quality). The danger zone FSIS teaches for leftovers is 40°F to 140°F; keep hot food at 140°F or warmer.

Sources: FSIS Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart (last updated Apr 14, 2025); FDA Safe Food Handling; FoodSafety.gov safe minimum internal temperatures (date last reviewed November 21, 2024); FDA Cooking (Food Safety for Moms-to-Be); FSIS Leftovers and Food Safety (last updated Jul 31, 2020).

Quick workflow

  1. Season the salmon generously with salt on all sides (Anova and Serious Eats). Both prefer a dry brine: bag the salted fillets and rest them in the refrigerator at least 30 minutes and up to overnight so the salt can firm the flesh. Add a couple of teaspoons of olive oil per fillet, and only gentle aromatics (thyme, dill, parsley, thinly sliced shallots, or citrus zest). Do not add large chunks of food or acidic ingredients, which can damage shape or texture.
  2. Preheat the bath to the texture temperature you want from the Anova/Serious Eats chart, and let it reach temperature before adding the salmon.
  3. Remove the air with a vacuum sealer or the water-displacement method, fully submerge, and cook within the timing range: 30 to 45 minutes for fillets about 1 inch thick or less, or 45 minutes to 1 hour for fillets up to about 2 inches. Handle the fish carefully after the bath — Anova and Serious Eats both note that cooked salmon is very delicate.
  4. Pat dry, then serve as-is, chill and serve cold, or sear skin-side down in a thin layer of shimmering oil in a skillet over medium-high heat until the skin is browned and crisp, about 1½ minutes, and briefly kiss the second side (Anova, Serious Eats). Verify with an instant-read thermometer if you are aiming for the USDA/FDA consumer minimum or a Baldwin Table 3.1 pasteurization hold.

Anova compared water brines and dry brines and preferred dry-brining with salt alone for convenience; a half hour was “the magic number” in that test. Baldwin uses a different albumin-control step in his mi-cuit recipe: a 10% salt-water brine for 10 minutes, then rinse and pat dry. Those are two named methods, not one combined brine.
